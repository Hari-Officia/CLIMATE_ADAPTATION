P000: Quantum-Assisted Multi-Agent Decision Support for Climate Adaptation: A Comparative Literature Survey and Research Framework

P002: Department of Information Technology, Rajalakshmi Engineering College, Chennai, India
P003: Supervisor: Mrs. K. Indumathi
P004: Abstract
P005: Climate change produces spatially heterogeneous hazards, including floods, droughts, and heatwaves, so adaptation planning requires decision support that connects risk prediction, spatial context, and the selection of interventions. This paper reviews 20 literature records (18 unique studies after duplicate removal) on machine-learning risk prediction, geospatial analysis, multi-agent decision support, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), climate adaptation, and classical and quantum optimization, and identifies the methodological approaches that dominate each stream. Within the reviewed corpus, no single study was identified that integrates multi-hazard risk prediction, district-level spatial assessment, explainable prioritization, and constrained adaptation-portfolio optimization. Guided by the reviewed approaches, we implement and benchmark representative methods on a Tamil Nadu dataset of 228,950 district-day records (38 districts, 53 model features): XGBoost-based flood, drought, and heatwave risk prediction, and the selection of K = 5 strategies from 14 canonical adaptation strategies by exact enumeration, mixed-integer linear programming (MILP), greedy search, simulated annealing, and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA; 1,900 runs, 1,024 shots per run). QAOA reached a best observed optimality gap of 0.0267 (mean 0.0845). The results are related back to the reviewed literature, and the remaining integration gap motivates a proposed framework that links risk prediction, spatial context, adaptation priority, strategy applicability, optimization, and explainable output. Quantum advantage is not established in the current experiments; the quantum results come from simulation and hardware-calibrated simulation, and physical IBM QPU execution has not been verified.
P006: Keywords: climate adaptation; climate risk assessment; multi-agent systems; geospatial analysis; explainable artificial intelligence; machine learning; QUBO; QAOA; decision support; climate resilience.
P007: 1. Introduction
P008: Climate adaptation requires planners to decide which protective measures to implement under changing environmental conditions, across locations that differ in exposure, vulnerability, and physical characteristics, and within limited resources. Floods, droughts, and heatwaves affect such locations unevenly, so adaptation decisions are more meaningfully framed at the level of individual districts than of entire regions.
P009: Several research directions relevant to this problem are established. Machine-learning studies forecast climate variables and hazards with increasing skill [9], [12], [16]; geospatial methods resolve hazard susceptibility at fine spatial scale [13], [14]; multi-agent systems support policy generation, research assistance, and stakeholder deliberation [1], [2], [5], [7]; XAI methods make model outputs interpretable [12]; adaptation and resilience studies address planning under uncertainty and resource limits [6], [17], [18]; multi-agent and reinforcement-learning methods address allocation problems [8], [10], [11]; and quantum computing has been proposed for climate modelling and optimization [3], [4]. These directions have largely developed independently. Estimating risk is only the first step: planners must still translate predicted risk into interpretable, resource-constrained choices among adaptation measures.
P010: This paper therefore follows a literature-to-experiment logic. Section 2 reviews the literature and identifies the dominant methodological approaches and the remaining gap. Sections 3 and 4 construct a study dataset and operationalize the approaches that could be implemented on it, and Section 5 reports the results and relates them back to the literature. Section 6 then proposes a framework that integrates the fragmented capabilities. The paper addresses three research questions.
P011: RQ1. How do existing studies address climate-risk prediction, spatial analysis, multi-agent coordination, explainability, adaptation planning, and optimization?
P012: RQ2. To what extent do existing studies integrate these capabilities into a single climate-adaptation decision workflow?
P013: RQ3. When representative literature-derived methods are implemented on a common Tamil Nadu dataset and a shared strategy-portfolio formulation, what do they show, and how do classical and quantum-assisted optimizers compare?
P014: The contributions are as follows. (C1) Section 2 synthesizes the reviewed literature into thematic streams, summarizes all 20 records with their gaps (Table 1), and compares the streams against a common set of capabilities (Table 2). (C2) Sections 3 and 4 construct the study dataset and map each literature theme to an experimental component, stating which approaches were implemented and which were not (Table 3). (C3) Section 5 reports the benchmark results for risk prediction and for classical versus QAOA optimization, interpreted against the literature. (C4) Section 6 derives a research framework from the remaining integration gap, and Section 7 states the limitations.

P017: 2.1 Climate-Risk Prediction and Forecasting
P018: Machine learning is the dominant approach to climate-risk prediction in the reviewed corpus. Bihlo et al. [9] apply transformer networks to sub-seasonal precipitation forecasting from reanalysis data and report better accuracy than LSTM and CNN baselines. Nandan and Gupta [12] combine Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM models for multi-hazard early risk forecasting, and [16] couples deep learning with integrated assessment models to improve long-term climate-impact prediction and policy evaluation. Geospatial machine-learning models extend prediction to flood susceptibility [14].
P019: The common limitation is scope: these studies estimate a hazard or climate variable and stop there, leaving the step from predicted risk to adaptation choices to the user, and two of them are single-hazard [9], [14]. This separation between prediction and decision-making motivates the risk-prediction experiment (Section 4.2), in which hazard probabilities are inputs to, rather than the end of, the decision workflow.
P020: 2.2 GIS and Geospatial Climate-Risk Analysis
P021: GIS supplies the spatial layer that turns climate and hazard information into location-specific assessments. Li et al. [14] combine satellite imagery, GIS data, and terrain information for high-resolution flood mapping and report finer spatial resolution than traditional mapping, while Lu et al. [13] integrate GIS with XGBoost and deep neural networks for carbon-storage site selection, illustrating GIS–ML integration for spatial screening. An agent-based evacuation study [17] identifies GIS-based hazard mapping as a planned extension.
P022: In these studies GIS acts mainly as a mapping or screening component. A decision-support workflow additionally requires spatial outputs to be linked to exposure, vulnerability, adaptation priority, and strategy selection. This motivates the district-level spatial analysis in Section 4.3.
P023: 2.3 Multi-Agent Systems for Climate Decision Support
P024: Multi-agent systems decompose complex climate decisions into specialized analytical roles. ClimatePolicyGen [1] uses five specialized agents to integrate environmental, socio-economic, and infrastructure time series into region-specific policies, and ClimateAgents [2] combines data retrieval, statistical analysis, hypothesis generation, and automated reasoning for social-climate research. A role-based model [5] assigns expert, evaluator, disseminator, and decision-maker roles to support adaptation deliberation across living labs, and a retrieval-augmented multi-agent system [7] supports wildfire adaptation planning. A further group applies multi-agent reinforcement learning to allocation and equity problems: post-hazard relief distribution [8], climate-policy equity [10], and a survey of resource-allocation methods [11].
P025: These studies establish the value of agent specialization and collaborative processing. Within the reviewed corpus, however, multi-agent systems emphasize policy generation, deliberation, research assistance, or allocation, and none combines multi-hazard prediction, spatial risk assessment, adaptation prioritization, and formal strategy optimization. In the present paper, multi-agent processing is treated as the coordination layer of the proposed framework (Section 6) and is not experimentally evaluated.
P026: 2.4 Explainable Artificial Intelligence for Climate Risk
P027: Explainability addresses the need to understand why a model produces a given prediction. In the reviewed corpus, the principal contribution in this stream is [12], which pairs Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM models with SHAP, LIME, and counterfactual explanations for multi-hazard risk forecasting; its PRISMA-guided review (2018–2025) reports improved trust and usability of climate-risk models but persistent gaps in scalability and multi-hazard integration. Other studies name XAI as a planned extension for flood mapping [14] and for reinforcement-learning-based mitigation planning [15], while multi-agent studies report interpretable reports or improved transparency at the level of reasoning and deliberation [2], [5], [7] rather than feature-level attribution.
P028: Explainability in this literature is applied at the prediction stage. What is missing is a link from feature-level contributions to risk drivers, decision context, and the eventual choice of adaptation strategies. Section 4.4 therefore keeps XAI as a distinct experimental component that interprets predictions and decision factors without determining the selected strategy.
P029: 2.5 Climate Adaptation and Resilience Decision Support
P030: Adaptation research increasingly addresses decisions under uncertainty, stakeholder interaction, and resource limits. The reviewed studies include role-based adaptation deliberation [5], game-theoretic multi-agent evaluation of water and environmental management scenarios under climate change [6], post-hazard recovery [8], agent-based evacuation modelling [17], and decision support for adaptation in agriculture and natural resources that emphasizes data accessibility and stakeholder participation [18]. Mitigation-oriented work on reinforcement-learning policy simulation [15] and integrated assessment [16] addresses related planning problems at policy scale.
P031: Many of these studies are scenario-specific, stakeholder-oriented, or tied to one resource domain, and few couple predictive risk models with formal selection of multiple adaptation strategies. Section 4.5 addresses this coupling by representing adaptation planning as a constrained strategy-selection problem.
P032: 2.6 Classical Optimization for Climate and Resource Planning
P033: Optimization appears in the corpus mainly as allocation or policy learning: game-theoretic resource allocation [6], proximal-policy-optimization-based relief distribution [8], multi-objective reinforcement learning for policy trade-offs [10], reinforcement-learning mitigation planning [15], and GIS–ML site selection [13]; the survey in [11] covers MADQN, MAPPO, MAAC, and MADDPG. These methods show the importance of representing resource limits and competing objectives explicitly.
P034: They do not, however, formulate adaptation as selecting a constrained portfolio of interventions with portfolio-size, conflict, dependency, and interaction constraints, nor do they provide exact reference solutions against which approximate solvers can be judged. Section 4.6 adopts that binary-portfolio formulation, with exact enumeration and MILP as reference methods.
P035: 2.7 Quantum Computing for Climate Applications
P036: Quantum computing has been explored for climate modelling and optimization. Because the role of the quantum component differs across applications, the corpus is discussed under three sub-streams.
P037: 2.7.1 Quantum Machine Learning
P038: Schwabe et al. [3] develop quantum machine-learning models based on quantum neural networks for climate modelling and Earth-system prediction on high-dimensional data, and report promising gains in computational efficiency and prediction accuracy. Their stated future work is validation of hybrid quantum-classical models on large real-world datasets and deployment on fault-tolerant hardware. The work targets prediction rather than adaptation decisions; the present study does not use QAOA or any other quantum method for climate prediction.
P039: 2.7.2 Quantum and Quantum-Inspired Feature Selection
P040: No record in the reviewed corpus addresses quantum or quantum-inspired feature selection. This sub-stream is therefore not synthesized here, and the QUBO formulation in Section 4.7 encodes strategy-portfolio selection, not feature selection.
P041: 2.7.3 Quantum Optimization for Decision Problems
P042: The review in [4] surveys quantum computing for climate modelling, optimization, uncertainty quantification, and environmental decision support, and concludes that hardware scalability and quantum noise remain major challenges. A mitigation-planning study [16] lists quantum computing as a future direction. No record in the corpus evaluates QAOA or another quantum optimizer on a constrained adaptation-portfolio problem, and the corpus contains no primary QAOA or QUBO methodology paper ([INSERT VERIFIED QAOA/QUBO METHOD REFERENCE]). Consequently, quantum methods should be tested against well-defined classical baselines rather than assumed to be advantageous. The present study belongs to this third sub-stream, and Sections 4.7 and 5.3 evaluate it accordingly.

P222: 3.4 Adaptation Strategy Representation
P223: A structured strategy library represents candidate interventions: 14 canonical adaptation strategies across 10 adaptation domains. Applicability rules determine which strategies are relevant to a hazard and location context and can therefore enter the optimization. The library also carries 45 evidence links that associate strategies with supporting sources; these links are not strategies and are not counted as such.
P224: 3.5 Integration and Processing
P225: Hazard probabilities from the prediction experiment are combined with district-level spatial context and with the applicable subset of the strategy library. The resulting adaptation-priority and strategy-applicability information forms the input to the portfolio optimization, in which classical and quantum-assisted solvers operate on identical objective and constraint definitions.

P231: 4.2 Risk-Prediction Experiment
P232: Machine-learning studies reviewed in Section 2.1 demonstrate the use of supervised learning for climate-hazard prediction, including transformer networks [9] and Random Forest, XGBoost, and LSTM models [12]. Based on this literature, the present study evaluates an XGBoost-based multi-hazard risk-prediction workflow. P393: 5.4 Comparison with Existing Research and Remaining Gap
P394: The experiments operationalize the approaches of Table 3 that could be run on the study data: tree-based hazard prediction [12], district-level spatial representation [13], [14], constrained portfolio selection motivated by adaptation studies [5], [6], [18], classical reference and baseline solvers, and a QUBO/QAOA formulation motivated by the quantum literature [3], [4]. Two observations follow. First, the prediction results confirm that hazard risk can be ranked well on this dataset, but the rare-event flood result and the possible leakage in the other two hazards show that headline scores are insufficient evidence of decision-ready risk estimates. Second, the optimization results show that the strategy-portfolio problem can be posed identically to classical and quantum-assisted solvers, which the reviewed optimization literature [6], [8], [10], [11], [15] does not do for a constrained adaptation portfolio.
P395: What remains open is the integration itself. The prediction and optimization stages are evaluated here as separate experiments; a quantitative end-to-end evaluation in which explained, spatially contextualized risk drives the selected portfolio, and in which selected portfolios are validated against observed adaptation outcomes, is not reported in this paper. This remaining gap motivates the framework in Section 6.

P445: 7. Limitations and Future Work
P446: Data. Hazard labels are rare, particularly for floods; the near-perfect drought and heatwave scores may reflect target–feature leakage; temporal generalization has not been shown; and long-term outcome data on how adaptation strategies perform are limited. Spatial. Coastal and compound-hazard processes are not modelled in detail, and fine-scale risk requires further validation. Optimization. The canonical strategy space contains 14 strategies, and scaling to larger QUBO instances remains computationally challenging. Hardware. Physical IBM QPU execution has not been verified; the quantum evidence is from simulation and hardware-calibrated simulation. Scientific. Quantum advantage is not established in the current experiments.
P447: Future work will (i) incorporate longer-term observed adaptation outcomes to test whether selected strategies reduce risk; (ii) integrate higher-resolution infrastructure and vulnerability data; (iii) model compound and coastal hazards in more spatial and physical detail; (iv) study larger QUBO problems to assess QAOA scaling; (v) conduct authenticated physical quantum-hardware experiments; (vi) implement and compare the literature-identified prediction models not run here, such as transformer and LSTM models [9], [12]; and (vii) evaluate the integrated workflow end to end.
P448: 8. Conclusion
P449: This paper reviewed 20 literature records (18 unique studies) across climate-risk prediction, GIS, multi-agent decision support, explainable AI, climate adaptation, and classical and quantum optimization. The literature shows substantial progress within each stream but fragmentation between prediction, spatial assessment, interpretability, adaptation planning, and portfolio optimization. Guided by the methods identified in the literature, the study benchmarked XGBoost-based hazard prediction on a 38-district Tamil Nadu dataset and compared exact, MILP, greedy, simulated-annealing, and QAOA solvers on a 14-strategy, K = 5 portfolio problem, with QAOA reaching a best observed gap of 0.0267 over 1,900 runs.
P450: These results support the feasibility of the benchmarking workflow. They do not establish quantum advantage, the prediction scores require leakage and temporal validation, and physical IBM QPU execution has not been verified. The remaining gap is the quantitative integration of explained risk with strategy selection, which the proposed framework is intended to address in future work.
